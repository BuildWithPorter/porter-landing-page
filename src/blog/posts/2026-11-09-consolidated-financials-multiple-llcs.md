Most owners don't set out to run a dozen companies. You start one business, a second one makes sense for liability reasons, and then a holding company and a property LLC follow, each with its own bank account and books. Each decision made sense, although together they mean nobody can quickly tell you how the group did last month.

We recently onboarded a company with 11 entities whose books were months behind. Two weeks later they had statements ready for a proposal to one of their own clients. The reasons their books fell behind are the same ones we see in almost every group of companies.

## Why do the books for multiple LLCs drift apart?

Each company usually has its own accounting file and bank feeds, so small differences add up. One company records a shared software bill in full, another records nothing, and a third books a transfer from a sister company as revenue. Since every file looks fine on its own, the problems only show up when someone adds them together, usually because a lender or client asked.

## What do intercompany transactions do to consolidated financials?

When one of your companies pays another for rent, management fees or a loan, the money never leaves the group, although it shows up in both sets of books. If you just add the statements up, that rent appears as revenue in one company and an expense in the other, and the loan shows up as an asset and a liability at the same time. So the group looks bigger than it is, and the balance sheet carries amounts that only exist between your own companies.

Consolidation fixes this by eliminating those transactions, which only works if both sides agree. If one company recorded a loan repayment and the other didn't, the elimination leaves a gap, and that gap is usually the first thing a reviewer finds.

## What do lenders and clients need to see?

A lender, buyer or large client is asking whether the business behind the deal is stable, so they want statements that tie to the bank, show each company and the group, and reconcile from one to the other without unexplained differences.

## How do you consolidate books across multiple companies?

You need a routine that runs every month.

1. **Map the structure.** Write down who owns what and which companies roll up into another, so the consolidated view matches the legal one.
2. **Close every company on the same schedule.** Reconcile each bank and card account and close each company through the same date.
3. **Tag every intercompany transaction.** Record the sister company as the counterparty on both sides, so each payment can be matched to its other half.
4. **Split shared costs with a written rule.** Decide once how costs that serve several companies get divided, and apply that rule every month.
5. **Eliminate and check.** Remove matched intercompany activity, review anything left unmatched, and confirm each company still ties to its own trial balance.
