A few weeks after you close, the seller's bookkeeper is usually winding down, and the bank starts asking for its first quarterly package. The usual approach is to keep the seller's file, patch it at year-end, and footnote the gaps for the lender, which leaves you explaining numbers you have not really checked. A better order is to rebuild trust in those numbers in the first 100 days, then close every month from there.

## What should you set up for accounting right after you buy a company?

Start by making sure the accounts are actually yours to see. Get your own logins to every bank and card account, to payroll, and to the accounting file, and write down who else still has access, including the seller and the people they worked with. Some of them may stay on for a short handoff, but you want your own access first, because you are now responsible for those accounts. Then decide which systems you are keeping. Judge the file after you have access and before you change tools.

## How do you check that the books you inherited are right?

Reconcile every bank and card account from the closing date forward. Clearing accounts for card payments or payroll should sit at zero once payments have settled. We have seen a card-payment clearing account stay off for months because the same payment was counted twice. Then tie the opening balance sheet back to the purchase agreement, so the balances you agreed to buy match the deal, or you have a written explanation for the difference. Bankers often need those books cleaned up before they will lend, so a file kept for the seller's tax return may not be ready for your first loan package.

## Should you report on cash or accrual after an acquisition?

Many small companies file taxes on a cash basis, while the lender wants to see accrual, so you have to decide which set of books you will actually run. Cash tells you what hit the bank, and accrual tells you what you earned and what you owe. If you keep both views in two files, you end up footnoting the differences each time the lender asks, which is what we see when books have been mixed for years. A middle path is modified cash with accrual receivables, so you still recognize what customers owe you. Watch inventory closely, because if the seller expensed purchases in full instead of carrying them as an asset, your margins will not match the numbers you used to buy the company.

## Who owns bills and invoices once the seller is gone?

Once the seller is gone, bills and invoices often have no owner. The person who approved payments or chased customers may have been the seller, or may already have left. Put a name on both lists early, including who approves a large or disputed bill and who follows up when a customer is late. Also check where payments are going, because customers sometimes keep paying the seller's old account, or a deposit lands in a personal or related-company account and never gets tied back to the invoice. Until someone owns that matching, your receivables look worse than they are, and cash you already collected never shows up in the company you just bought.

## What does a monthly close look like by day 100?

By day 100, you want a close you can repeat every month. Reconcile every bank and card account, clear the bills and invoices that should no longer be open, and explain the swings versus last month. Write a short owner report with cash and collections for your lender. If the books you inherited were behind, finish that catch-up in order before you treat the new months as closed, as we walk through in [how to catch up on bookkeeping](/blog/how-to-catch-up-on-bookkeeping). If you have a holding company or sister companies, each entity should stand on its own before you combine anything. That is the same order we use for [consolidated financials across multiple LLCs](/blog/consolidated-financials-multiple-llcs) and on our [multi-entity](/multi-entity) page.

## FAQ

### How long does finance setup take after an acquisition?

It depends on the books you inherited and how quickly we can get access to the banks, cards, and statements. Once we have that access, we can give you a timeline for your specific setup.

### Should I keep the seller's bookkeeper?

If they know the accounts and can stay through the handoff, that can help, because they often hold context that is not in the file. If they are leaving, get the logins and the history from them first.

### Does my lender want cash or accrual?

Many lenders want to see accrual, even when the company files taxes on a cash basis, so ask what they expect and pick a method you can produce every month.

### What should my first monthly report include?

It should include a balance sheet and profit and loss that tie to reconciled accounts, a view of open bills and invoices, and a short explanation of the swings versus last month or the deal.
